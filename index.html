Quick reminders

  • Quantum numbers: n = 1, 2, 3… · l = 0 to n−1 (s=0, p=1, d=2, f=3) · ml = −l to +l · ms = ±½
  • Orbitals in shell n: n² · Max electrons: 2n²
  • Filling order: 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p 5s 4d 5p…
  • Exceptions: Cr = [Ar] 4s¹ 3d⁵ · Cu = [Ar] 4s¹ 3d¹⁰
  • Transition metal ions: remove 4s electrons before 3d.
  • Hund's rule: one electron in each orbital before pairing.
  • Paramagnetic = has unpaired electrons · Diamagnetic = all paired.
  • Atomic radius: decreases across a period, increases down a group.
  • Ionization energy: increases across a period, decreases down a group.
  • Isoelectronic species: more protons = smaller (e.g. Cl⁻ > Ar > K⁺).
  • Successive ionization energies: look for the big jump — the number of electrons before it = valence electrons = group.
  • ΔE sign: moving up a level (n increases) = positive, photon absorbed. Moving down = negative, photon emitted.
  • Phase changes: temperature stays constant — the energy breaks intermolecular forces instead of raising kinetic energy.

12) Ethanol vapor pressure

Ethanol has a vapor pressure of 7.87 kPa at 25.0 °C and ΔHvap = 42.3 kJ/mol.

a) What is the vapor pressure of ethanol at 50.0 °C? (in kPa)
Isolate P2 first: P2 = P1 · e−(ΔH/R)(1/T₂ − 1/T₁)
T₁ = 25.0 + 273.15 = 298.15 K, T₂ = 50.0 + 273.15 = 323.15 K
ΔH/R = 42300 J/mol ÷ 8.314 J/(mol·K) = 5087.8 K
1/T₂ − 1/T₁ = −2.59478 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹
exponent = −(5087.8)(−2.59478 × 10⁻⁴) = 1.320166
P₂ = 7.87 kPa × e1.320166 = 7.87 × 3.744057 = 29.5 kPa
Common mistakes: using °C instead of K, or leaving ΔH in kJ.
b) At what temperature (°C) does ethanol's vapor pressure reach 101.325 kPa? What is that temperature called?
Isolate 1/T₂: 1/T₂ = 1/T₁ − (R/ΔH) · ln(P₂/P₁)
ln(101.325 / 7.87) = ln(12.87484) = 2.555283
1/T₂ = 1/298.15 − 2.555283/5087.8 = 3.354016 × 10⁻³ − 5.02235 × 10⁻⁴ = 2.85178 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹
T₂ = 350.659 K → 350.659 − 273.15 = 77.5 °C
Since 101.325 kPa is 1 atm, this is the normal boiling point.
c) A sealed flask holds liquid ethanol and its vapor at 25.0 °C. The pressure stops changing, but molecules still leave and enter the liquid. Explain and name the condition.
Dynamic equilibrium. Molecules are still evaporating and condensing, but the two rates are equal, so the number of molecules in the vapor (and the pressure) stays constant. Nothing has stopped — the steady pressure is the vapor pressure of ethanol at 25.0 °C.

13) Selenium (Z = 34) and cobalt (Z = 27)

a) Full electron configuration of Se, noble-gas shorthand, and number of valence electrons.
Full: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s² 3d¹⁰ 4p⁴
Shorthand: [Ar] 4s² 3d¹⁰ 4p⁴

14) Define and relate: amplitude, wavelength, frequency, photon energy

a) Amplitude: the height of a wave from the midline to a crest. It sets the intensity (brightness) — more photons per second, not more energetic ones.

b) Wavelength (λ): distance between two identical points on a wave (crest to crest). Measured in m (nm for visible light). Sets the color.

c) Frequency (ν): number of wave cycles passing a point each second, in Hz (s⁻¹). For light in a vacuum, c = λ · ν.

d) Photon energy: the energy of one packet of light, E = h · ν. It doesn't change when the light gets brighter.

e) How they relate: Wavelength and frequency are inversely proportional (c = λν). Photon energy is directly proportional to frequency (E = hν) and inversely proportional to wavelength (E = hc/λ). Short λ = high ν = high photon energy. Amplitude is the odd one out — it only tells you how many photons arrive, not how much energy each one has. That's why in the photoelectric effect, dim blue light ejects electrons but bright red light doesn't.
